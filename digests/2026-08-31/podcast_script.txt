[BREAK]

Maya: Start with the Fourier transform toolbox, because there's a long history behind it. Sam, why has this been stuck?

Sam: Because Shor's algorithm is, at bottom, a Fourier transform over a group where everything commutes — adding numbers. The dream for twenty-odd years has been the non-commuting version: graph isomorphism, certain lattice problems, all sit inside what's called the hidden subgroup problem over groups like permutations or matrix groups. And the first thing you need is a fast circuit for the Fourier transform on that group. We have efficient ones for a handful of families. For everything else, the generic recipe costs time proportional to the size of the group — which is exponential in the input.

Maya: So the group has a billion elements, you pay a billion.

Sam: Roughly. What this group does — Bruinsma, Posta, Stokvis, Grinko and Ozols — is give two systematic recipes for building a group's transform out of a subgroup's. One uses Mackey theory, where the bookkeeping is organized by double cosets; the other uses Clifford theory. Then they cash them in. The headline case is the group of invertible two-by-two matrices over a finite field, and their circuit's cost grows with the number of digits of the field size, not the field size itself. That's an exponential improvement over anything previously written down for it.

Maya: And the second recipe?

Sam: Wreath products — a group of local objects with permutations acting on top. There the cost is inherited from whatever transform you have for the small base group. Previously you needed that base group to be tiny, polynomial in the number of copies. That restriction just falls away.

Maya: Okay, but what does this buy us? Is there an algorithm at the other end of it?

Sam: Not in this paper, and they don't pretend otherwise. There's no end-to-end gate count and no application. It's approximate — you pay in circuit size for accuracy — and the circuit calls a coherent discrete logarithm as a subroutine, which is essentially Shor's machinery run reversibly. So these aren't elementary circuits, they're circuits built on top of another quantum algorithm.

Maya: That's a real caveat. If your Fourier transform needs a discrete log, the accounting gets circular fast.

Sam: It's fair, though the discrete log is cheap in the relevant sense. The limit I'd flag harder is that everything here is rank two. Two-by-two matrices work partly because the Bruhat decomposition gives exactly two pieces to glue, and the subgroup chain has no multiplicities. Three-by-three is open.

Maya: And the gadget I opened with?

Sam: That's my favourite bit. Diagonalizing the one genuinely hard block requires routing a field element according to whether a particular quadratic splits over the field. Split values feed one family of representations, irreducible ones feed another. It's a quantum circuit branching on arithmetic irreducibility, coherently. Read this if you hunt hidden-subgroup structure; the two recipes are templates, not one-offs.

[BREAK]

Maya: Paper two, from an overlapping crew, and the surprise is that a published complexity claim doesn't survive being compiled.

Sam: Right. Bruinsma, Grinko and Ozols take the two known constructions of the Fourier transform over the permutation group and actually push them down to one- and two-qubit gates. The newer of the two, Kawano and Sekigawa's, was advertised as scaling with the cube of the number of objects being permuted. When you compile it honestly, it picks up an extra factor of the square root of that number.

Maya: Where did the missing cost hide?

Sam: In a multi-qubit embedding gate everyone assumed decomposed into linearly many gates. It's linear in depth — you can do it in parallel — but the total gate count is larger. Depth and count got conflated, and that propagated into the literature.

Maya: And the older construction?

Sam: Beals's, from the nineties, conceptually simpler. With a smarter choice of coset representatives — powers of one long cycle — consecutive rotation gates telescope into products of neighbouring swaps, and the count drops from the fourth power to the cube, in both gates and depth. So the older, simpler thing is now the fastest known. That did surprise me.

Maya: Why should anyone outside representation-theory circles care about a half-power?

Sam: Because this transform is the dominant cost in a whole class of algorithms — estimating Kronecker coefficients, plethysm coefficients, Littlewood–Richardson numbers — and it's also the engine of the best Schur transform when the local dimension is large. When your speedup is polynomial, the exponent is the whole argument. Moving from three-and-a-half to three shifts where the quantum-versus-classical crossover actually sits.

Maya: What's still soft here?

Sam: Two things. All of it hides polylogarithmic factors, and coherent arithmetic is charged at polylog cost, so real resource counts — constants, actual gates on actual hardware — remain unknown. And there's no lower bound, so "best known" is not "optimal." Their own space analysis shows the qubit count sits well above the information-theoretic minimum, which for these path labels is a Yamanouchi word.

Maya: So it's bookkeeping. But bookkeeping that changed a published exponent and dethroned the newer method.

Sam: And it uses the same Mackey machinery as the toolbox paper — that's how they derive a cleaner recursion and delete two whole gates from the newer construction. Same group, same week, same hammer. If you're implementing Schur transforms, this is the reference now.

[BREAK]

Maya: Next one starts with something that sounds like a contradiction. Magic — the resource that puts you beyond Clifford circuits — depends on which local basis you choose. So how can you talk about the magic in a shared state at all?

Sam: That's exactly the tension Piotr Sierant is resolving. If Alice rotates her own qubits, she can move magic around, so part of any state's magic is just an artifact of axes. The part that isn't — the part stored in the correlations — is defined as the magic minimized over all local unitaries. Beautiful definition, hopeless to compute. Before this, people could do it for two qubits.

Maya: And now?

Sam: Now there's a closed form, for one standard measure — the stabilizer fidelity, how well your state can be approximated by a stabilizer state. And the answer depends on nothing but the entanglement spectrum. Concretely: how evenly the Schmidt weight is spread across doublings of rank.

Maya: Walk me through why the entanglement spectrum is the right object.

Sam: Because stabilizer entanglement is quantized. Across any cut, a stabilizer state is just some number of Bell pairs plus unentangled spectators — so its entanglement spectrum is perfectly flat, with equal steps. Asking how magical your state is becomes asking how badly its spectrum fails to look flat over any window whose width is a factor of two in rank. Once you see that, the optimization over local unitaries collapses into a maximum over a handful of partial sums of sorted Schmidt coefficients.

Maya: What does that give you in numbers?

Sam: Bounds that are almost comically tame. Nonlocal magic is at most logarithmic in the entanglement entropy — so any area-law state has a constant amount, and a critical chain grows only like the logarithm of the logarithm of its length. Haar-random states sit below a quarter of a bit. Random singlet states are exactly zero.

Maya: Zero is a strong statement. Those states are highly entangled.

Sam: They are, and that's the point — this diagnostic separates states that entanglement entropy alone cannot tell apart. And there's a second half I find genuinely surprising: this same quantity, up to a sharp additive constant, is the known criterion for universal embezzlement — being a catalyst from which any entangled state can be borrowed. So a family of states embezzles universally exactly when its nonlocal magic diverges. A computational resource and a catalytic one turn out to be the same measurement.

Maya: What's the honest scope? "One standard measure" is doing work in that sentence.

Sam: It is. Exactness holds for the stabilizer fidelity. For the stabilizer Rényi entropy version, the popular one, he only brackets it within constant factors, and that leans on an unpublished companion result. The measure itself isn't an entanglement monotone, isn't additive. The critical-chain law comes from a smoothed conformal field theory spectrum, not from finite chains. Mixed states are open.

Maya: Still, if it's a function of the bond spectrum, DMRG people get it for free.

Sam: Free — they already store those numbers — and it's accessible experimentally through entanglement spectroscopy, with a stated robustness bound. What's missing is a sample-efficient estimator for those ordered partial sums. That's the obvious next paper.

[BREAK]

Maya: Now the counterpoint to the whole day. On Friday we covered a decoder for quantum expander codes where the entire content was a theorem — provable correction, hand-built. This one goes the other way.

Sam: Completely. Concatenated codes — codes inside codes — are attractive because you can pack many logical qubits into relatively few physical ones. But historically every family needed its own hand-crafted decoder, and those decoders process each level separately, each error type separately, and truncate their search to stay fast. Correlations get thrown away.

Maya: So what's the replacement?

Sam: Wu, Zhang, Wu and Huang build one graph for any concatenated stabilizer code. A node for each Pauli type at every address and every level of the hierarchy. Then three kinds of constraint tie it together: the stabilizer checks you'd expect, a hierarchy rule that a parent's error is the parity of its children's, and a compatibility rule linking the three Pauli types at each site. Soft confidences flow up, down, and sideways, and tiny neural networks learn only how to combine incoming evidence.

Maya: Learn the message rule, keep the graph physics. What comes out?

Sam: On the concatenated fifteen-qubit Hamming code, the depolarizing pseudo-threshold roughly doubles — from about six and a half percent to about twelve. And after fine-tuning on circuit-level data from a teleportation-based protected gate benchmark, it beats the dedicated many-hypercube decoder, which is a purpose-built competitor, by about seventeen times in logical failure rate at level four.

Maya: I want to push on the threshold claim. At three or four levels of concatenation, a "pseudo-threshold" is just where two curves cross, isn't it? It's not the asymptotic threshold.

Sam: Correct, and they say so. It's a finite-level crossing, and doubling it is a statement about the regime you'd actually build in, not about the code's ultimate limit. The circuit-level noise model also omits single-qubit gate errors and idling, which is a real omission. And their level-four state preparation isn't distance-preserving, so a weight-six error creates an error floor — at low physical error rates the seventeen-fold advantage shrinks to a factor of three or so.

Maya: Plus the comparison itself is slightly apples-to-oranges?

Sam: Their acceptance rule for the protected gate is strictly stricter than the reference's, so yes, the head-to-head deserves a footnote. And the thing nobody addresses: latency. Fixed-iteration message passing is at least predictable, but there's no runtime number in the paper, and for concatenated schemes the decoder has to keep up with the syndrome stream or the machine stalls.

Maya: Verdict?

Sam: The result I'd bet on isn't the number, it's the genericity. It handles non-CSS component codes, which most bespoke decoders can't touch. If decoder availability was gating exploration of the concatenated design space — and it was — this reopens it.

[BREAK]

Maya: Last paper, and it starts from a slightly embarrassing fact about the field's favourite practical codes.

Sam: The embarrassing fact being that bivariate bicycle codes — currently the leading candidates for low-density parity-check quantum memory — were found essentially by brute force. You sweep over pairs of defining polynomials, compute the parameters, keep the winners. We know good codes exist at given sizes. We can't design them.

Maya: And Sabo, Can and Marquis give you the design language.

Sam: In one regime, yes. When the code length and the field characteristic are coprime — the semisimple case — the underlying ring splits into independent frequency components, indexed by orbits under the Frobenius map. Then everything becomes a question of which components your two polynomials annihilate. The number of logical qubits equals twice the size of the region where both polynomials vanish. Orbits where only one vanishes contribute exactly nothing — they're homologically trivial.

Maya: So the dimension you can now read off by inspection instead of computing a rank.

Sam: Right, and they verify it against known codes from the literature without ever writing down a stabilizer matrix. They also get a new distance lower bound, built from colon ideals, which catches logical operators that live across both blocks — those were missed by earlier per-block bounds. And a "spectral slope" invariant that classifies which coordinate permutations are symmetries of the code, which is what you need to prove that the block-swap duality exists.

Maya: That duality matters for the fold-transversal gate story we touched on last week with transversal non-Clifford gates — symmetry of the code buys you cheap logic.

Sam: Exactly, and here it's unconditional rather than found by search. But I have to be blunt about the distance bound, because it's the whole game for a memory.

Maya: Go on.

Sam: In all three worked examples it's loose. In the worst case it certifies distance two for a code whose actual distance is at least twenty-six. One example needs an ad hoc argument just to get past distance three. So dimension is now designable; distance still isn't.

Maya: Which is awkward, because the paper's own framing is that the shared roots creating your logical qubits are the same thing weakening your distance.

Sam: That tension is stated cleanly, and I think that's the durable contribution — a first-principles reason why dimension and distance fight. Other limits: it's semisimple only, the non-semisimple case needs Gröbner machinery and is flagged open; the symmetry analysis specializes to prime fields; and the authors concede the stabilizer weights in their examples are probably infeasible for near-term hardware. No new code tables either — the systematic search is deferred.

Maya: So who reads it?

Sam: Anyone building code families rather than using them. And the thing to watch is whether the promised structured enumeration actually beats the brute-force sweeps it's meant to replace. Until that lands, this is a dictionary without a translated book.
