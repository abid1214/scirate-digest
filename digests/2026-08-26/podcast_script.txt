Coming up: a lower bound and its matching algorithm posted within one arXiv number of each other, a quantum code that finally breaks a nine-year record for applying a non-Clifford gate the easy way, amplitude estimation that stops quietly failing at the edges, and a way to teleport a gate for less than one unit of entanglement.

Maya: Four proofs of optimality and one code. Let's go.

[BREAK]

Maya: Start me with a puzzle, because this first one only makes sense as a puzzle.

Sam: Here's the puzzle. Suppose you have a quantum subroutine that gives you the right answer with, say, ninety percent probability, and you want it to be right with a lot more nines than that. The obvious move is: run it several times, take the majority vote. The cost goes up in proportion to the number of nines you want.

Maya: And usually you can do better than obvious.

Sam: Right — in search you can. If Grover hands you a candidate, you just check whether it's the item you wanted. Verification is cheap, so error suppression is cheap. The question was whether ground-state energy estimation has anything like that.

Maya: Set up the problem for people who don't live in chemistry algorithms.

Sam: You have a Hamiltonian, you want its lowest energy, and you're allowed a head start: a cheap trial state — Hartree-Fock, say — that comes with a promise that it has at least some modest overlap with the true ground state. That's the guiding state. The cost of the best known algorithm grows inversely with that overlap, inversely with the precision you demand, and linearly in the number of nines of reliability.

Maya: And Somma and de Wolf show that's it. No cleverness available.

Sam: That's the result, and it's the first bound that's tight in all three of those knobs at the same time. Previously people could pin down one while holding the others fixed. So the naive majority-vote scaling turns out to be the truth.

Maya: My instinct is to say — fine, but does the hard case look anything like a molecule?

Sam: That's exactly the right pressure, and they answer half of it. The easy version of a lower bound uses a big degenerate ground space where the overlap is smeared across many states. They also do the harder, more physical version: a unique ground state with a genuine spectral gap, roughly three times the precision you're asking for. Same bound.

Maya: The other half being?

Sam: The other half they concede. In their hard instances, the guiding state carries essentially no information about the ground state beyond the bare overlap number. They flag that themselves — it suggests the whole "trial state with promised overlap" framing might be the wrong abstraction for chemistry. Real trial states know more than that.

Maya: So the theorem is airtight and the model might be the thing to attack.

Sam: Yes. And there's a nice bonus: there's a technique where you write the Hamiltonian as a sum of squares and get a square-root improvement in the precision dependence. They show that's near-optimal too, as a black-box method.

Maya: Limits I should hold onto?

Sam: They only count calls to the time evolution — gates, ancillas and state preparation are free, which makes the bound stronger, but leaves other cost models open. And they need the system dimension to be large, so this says nothing about small instances. Also: they disclose that the proofs were substantially AI-assisted, then simplified by hand.

Maya: We're going to come back to that disclosure line more than once today.

Sam: We are.

[BREAK]

Maya: Nine years. That's the number I want you to justify.

Sam: Two thousand seventeen, Hastings and Haah, then Haah in twenty eighteen. Since then, nobody improved the frontier for codes with a transversal T gate. Adam Wills just did.

Maya: Transversal meaning — you do the same thing to every qubit and something clean happens to the logical information.

Sam: Exactly. Apply the physical T gate to every single qubit in the block, and you get the logical T on every logical qubit, with no Clifford correction afterwards. That's the dream, because T is the gate that makes a quantum computer more than a classical simulation, and it's the expensive one.

Maya: Why is that so hard to arrange?

Sam: Because the arithmetic constraints are brutal. The rows of the code's generator matrix have to have weights in a specific residue class, pairwise overlaps divisible by four, triple overlaps even. Historically people got that by starting from Reed–Muller codes, which have loads of structure, and then punching holes in them to create logical qubits.

Maya: And the holes are where it gets hard.

Sam: The holes are where you lose control of the distance. Wills's move is to swap Reed–Muller for a much richer family — decreasing monomial codes — and then to choose the punctured coordinates so they have the same nested combinatorial structure as the code itself. When you do that, the minimum distance has an exact closed form. Not a bound. A formula.

Maya: And that formula is the actual product here.

Sam: I think so, yes. It's independently useful for polar codes and punctured evaluation codes. But the headline consequence is that he gets families where the number of logical qubits grows in proportion to the block size — constant rate — while the distance still grows.

Maya: Cash that out for magic state distillation.

Sam: In distillation you burn many noisy magic states to get one good one, and the number you burn scales like the number of nines you want raised to some exponent. That exponent used to be about two-thirds for transversal-T codes. Wills can push it as close to zero as you like.

Maya: Okay, here's where I get suspicious. Constant rate and growing distance sounds too good. How fast is it growing?

Sam: Slowly. It grows like two raised to the square root of the log of the block size. Which is sub-polynomial — it beats any constant, but at any block size you'd actually build, the distance is small.

Maya: So this is an asymptotic trophy, not a hardware win.

Sam: For now, largely yes, and he says so. The codes are also not low-density — the checks are dense, so this belongs in concatenated or hierarchical architectures where an expensive inner code is acceptable. And the genuinely good version — a transversal-T code with both rate and distance growing linearly — is still wide open.

Maya: Who should read it?

Sam: Anyone working on the hunt for low-density codes with native non-Clifford logic. The distance formula is a tool they don't currently have.

[BREAK]

Maya: Now the other half of that first story, because these two papers landed one arXiv number apart.

Sam: Same day, same problem, opposite directions. Jeffery and Witteveen give the algorithm that Somma and de Wolf prove you can't beat.

Maya: What was left to improve? I thought phase estimation was a solved textbook thing.

Sam: There was a stubborn logarithm. Every approach — standard phase estimation, the polynomial-filter methods — paid an extra factor of the log of one over the overlap. And there was a clear reason. Phase estimation has a noise floor, and a polynomial filter has ripples, and you have to push that floor below the size of your signal. Your signal is only as big as the overlap. So you pay.

Maya: And they just... don't pay it.

Sam: They don't. The cost is now inversely proportional to overlap times precision, full stop, which matches the bound exactly.

Maya: How? What's the trick?

Sam: Transducers, from Belovs and Jeffery. Here's the intuition I've settled on. Normally you build an algorithm by bolting together bounded-error modules, and at every joint you pay to clean up the error before handing off. A transducer is an object that composes exactly — you never have a bounded-error module in your hands until the very end, when you compile the whole thing into a circuit once.

Maya: So the logarithm was never intrinsic to the problem. It was an artifact of how we glue.

Sam: That's the claim, and I found that genuinely surprising. They build two standalone pieces — a transducer for amplitude amplification, which is a quantum walk on a star graph with one tunable edge weight, and one for phase estimation, a walk on weighted lines. Then they compose them and binary-search over the energy threshold.

Maya: How complicated is the resulting object?

Sam: That's the part that makes me think it might survive contact with a resource estimate. Two reflections and a counter. The constants are unoptimized, but the construction is small.

Maya: What's the asymmetry I saw in the summary — different costs for different oracles?

Sam: Nice detail. The calls to the time evolution scale as the optimal rate, but the calls to the state preparation unitary — the thing that makes your trial state — are much cheaper, only inversely with overlap and a couple of logs of precision. If preparing your guiding state is the expensive part in practice, that matters a lot.

Maya: Costs?

Sam: Ancillas. The workspace grows with the log of one over overlap times precision, which is more than the classical accounting, and shrinking it is open. There's an extra additive gate-count term. And they don't address full coherence — using this inside a larger superposition, which is what you'd need if it's a subroutine rather than the whole algorithm.

Maya: And that's the case the lower bound paper says is exactly where you can't cheaply reduce error.

Sam: Which is why the two belong together.

[BREAK]

Maya: Different world now. This one starts from hardware you can actually imagine having in a few years.

Sam: Right. Amplitude estimation is the engine behind quadratic speedups in Monte Carlo — pricing, chemistry integrals. Textbook version wants a deep coherent circuit, an ancilla register, and controlled Grover reflections. Early machines will hate all three.

Maya: So there's been a line of work trading coherence for repetition.

Sam: Yes — run shallower Grover circuits, take lots of noisy measurements, and do classical inference on the results. You get a dial between circuit depth and shot count. Erle and Koczor's contribution starts with a flaw in that line: the accuracy guarantees quietly break when the amplitude you're estimating is very close to zero or one.

Maya: Break how badly?

Sam: All the way down to classical sampling. You lose the quantum advantage entirely, in exactly the regime you care about most for things like certifying that a trial state really has the overlap it's promised.

Maya: Which is a lovely hook back to the first two papers.

Sam: It is. Their fix is almost disappointingly simple. Don't pick one circuit depth — sample depths from a probability distribution over a window of depths, collect the plus-one, minus-one outcomes, and fit a one-dimensional least-squares curve on a grid.

Maya: And that works at the edges because?

Sam: Because near the boundary the signal is weak but the variance of your measurements also vanishes, and once you track both carefully the fit still localizes. The earlier analyses used a Gaussian-approximation style argument that misses a cubic factor near the edge. This one doesn't.

Maya: What do they prove exactly?

Sam: They give a general admissibility criterion for which window distributions work — enough weight on shallow depths, plus a moment condition. Gaussian, Kaiser, uniform, linear, cubic windows all pass. And for any admissible window you get the optimal depth-versus-shots tradeoff, uniformly across every amplitude value, boundaries included, with no ancillas and no controlled Grover.

Maya: Here's my objection, and it's a real one. I looked at the constants in that theorem. They're astronomical — prefactors in the millions.

Sam: They are. The finite-sample bound as stated is not something you'd hand an implementer.

Maya: And the numerics don't rescue it either, because as far as I can tell they test one amplitude value in the middle of the range — not the boundary regime the entire theorem is about.

Sam: Correct, and that's the honest weakness. What I'd defend is two things. One, the scaling law is now established uniformly, which nobody had. Two, there's a clean negative result buried in there: the previous scheme had a special patch for the boundary case, and they show that patch is unnecessary and actually gives up the speedup wherever it's invoked.

Maya: Anything encouraging in the experiments?

Sam: One thing. At a fixed budget of about a million queries, the plain uniform window beat the Gaussian one and tracked the fundamental statistical limit closely. Which is a nice message for implementers: the fancy window isn't the good one.

Maya: What would you want to see next?

Sam: Numerics at an amplitude of, say, one in ten thousand. And a noise model, because none of this includes gate errors.

[BREAK]

Maya: Last one, and it's the result that made me put my coffee down.

Sam: Go on.

Maya: You have two distant labs. You want to apply a joint gate to a qubit in each, without letting them meet. And apparently, until now, even a gate that barely does anything — a controlled phase with a tiny angle, almost the identity — cost you a full unit of entanglement.

Sam: A full ebit. One perfect Bell pair, consumed, deterministically, exactly. Every known exact always-succeeding protocol paid that, regardless of how nonlocal the gate actually was.

Maya: Which is obviously silly.

Sam: It's obviously silly and it stayed open for a long time, because the natural proof techniques keep telling you a whole ebit is necessary. Schmitt and Sutter break it. Their protocol's entanglement cost shrinks smoothly to zero as the gate angle shrinks.

Maya: What does the resource look like? Because I can't hold zero point three of a Bell pair in my hand.

Sam: Fair challenge. It's a specific shared pure state with three terms in its Schmidt decomposition — not maximally entangled, an explicit lopsided thing. The number they quote is its entanglement entropy, which is the right currency if you're converting from Bell pairs in bulk. So the accounting is asymptotic, but the protocol itself is one-shot and deterministic.

Maya: How many rounds of talking?

Sam: Four rounds, four classical bits, two-way. Alice does a unitary and measures an ancilla, which splits the resource into two branches with probability exactly one half — and crucially, leaks nothing about her input. Bob applies a phase and a three-level Fourier transform so his measurement doesn't leak which branch he's in. Then Alice corrects.

Maya: And the correction being unitary is the constraint that fixes everything?

Sam: That's the elegant bit. The phases Bob needs are the intersection points of two circles in the complex plane, and that intersection condition is exactly the requirement that Alice's correction is a legal unitary. When the circles meet, the protocol exists.

Maya: Give me the payoff application.

Sam: A distributed Fourier transform. That circuit is dominated by controlled phases with tiny angles, and there are a lot of them — the entanglement budget used to grow with the square of the number of qubits. With this, on a split register, the total is a constant. About twelve ebits, forever, independent of size.

Maya: That's the number that got me. Where's the gap?

Sam: They have a converse from majorization, and it doesn't quite meet the protocol — there's a residual gap that grows with the angle, so the exact cost isn't pinned down. Optimality is proven only within their family of rank-three resource states; nobody's checked whether richer states do better. And for general two-qubit gates they decompose into three of these teleportations, which they say outright is suboptimal.

Maya: Verification status?

Sam: LLM-assisted construction, disclosed, and the algebra is branch-by-branch case analysis. I'd want someone to independently recheck it. But there's also a strong secondary theorem in there: if you insist on any fixed finite Schmidt rank, some angles still demand a full ebit. So the sub-ebit regime genuinely requires this kind of structure.

Sam: Mine is the logarithm that turned out not to exist. Jeffery and Witteveen removing a factor that everyone assumed was intrinsic to phase estimation, purely by changing how subroutines are glued together — I want to know what else that trick cleans up.
