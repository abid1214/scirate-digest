Five papers today: how many copies of a quantum state you need to check it's pure, a new way to measure how much noise destroys correlations across a network, a Lieb-Schultz-Mattis theorem for noisy dynamics, cellular automata meeting chiral phases of matter, and whether a photonic random number generator can still be trusted against an adversary who cheated before the experiment started.

[BREAK]

Maya: So here's something that shouldn't happen. You take a hard problem, you deliberately throw away structure to make the math tractable, and the sloppy version turns out to give the exactly right answer.

Sam: The problem is purity testing. Somebody hands you copies of an unknown quantum state and asks: is this a pure state, or is it far from pure? If you're allowed to load two copies into your quantum computer at once and interfere them — the swap test — you're done in a constant number of copies, independent of how big the system is.

Maya: And nobody can do that on real hardware.

Sam: Right, today you measure one copy, get a classical outcome, maybe choose your next measurement based on it, and repeat. In that world the cost blows up to roughly the square root of the dimension of the state space, which for a modest number of qubits is astronomically worse. That gap is one of the cleanest arguments that coherent multi-copy access is genuinely powerful.

Maya: But proving the gap is the hard part, I take it.

Sam: Brutally so. The standard tool is a "learning tree" analysis where you track every branch of every adaptive decision, or a martingale argument, and both are heavy. So people use a shortcut: instead of restricting to what one-copy-at-a-time strategies can do, you allow any measurement satisfying a partial-transposition condition. It's a strictly bigger class, and it's beautifully behaved algebraically.

Maya: Bigger class means weaker bound, presumably.

Sam: That was the folklore, and there was evidence for it — the best you could squeeze out was the fourth root of the dimension for purity, and the eighth root for the related task of checking whether a state is unentangled across a cut. Akresh and Beckey show the relaxation was never the problem. The analysis was.

Maya: What do they do differently?

Sam: A telescoping trick: they compare the true state to a fully random one by depolarizing one copy at a time, so a big messy difference becomes a sum of single-step differences. Then they lean on one classic identity — partially transposing the swap operator turns it into a projector onto a maximally entangled state, scaled by the dimension. That makes each term's trace norm computable exactly rather than bounded crudely.

Maya: And the payoff?

Sam: The square root of the dimension for purity testing, which matches the best known algorithms, so it's tight. And for product testing the same route jumps from the eighth root to the square root — a quartic improvement, also matching a known protocol. The whole argument is a couple of pages of symmetric-subspace facts.

Maya: My worry with slick proofs is coverage. Do these hold in the regimes people actually care about, or only when the dimension dwarfs the number of copies?

Sam: That's the nice part — no such restriction. It holds for every dimension and every number of copies above one, which the Schur-Weyl-based arguments usually can't promise. They also get the refined version where the bound grows as you demand finer error tolerance.

Maya: This connects to something we covered on Monday, the sample-to-query interconversion paper — the recurring lesson that physical copies of a state are a weaker resource than they look.

Sam: Same family of intuitions, yes. Honest limits: product testing is only done for a two-way split, nothing multipartite, and the error-tolerance scaling for that case isn't derived. And they flag their own open problem — stabilizer testing, where this method has not closed the gap. If you write learning lower bounds for a living, read this; the technique is reusable in an afternoon.

[BREAK]

Sam: Next one starts from a fact everyone in information theory internalizes on day one: noise can only make two states harder to tell apart. Never easier.

Maya: The data processing inequality. And the refinement is that you can often say how much harder — a shrink factor.

Sam: That's the contraction coefficient. Push both states through the channel and their distinguishability shrinks by at least that factor, every time. It controls mixing times, memory lifetimes, privacy guarantees, capacity bounds. But there's an embarrassment: the moment your system is correlated with something the noise doesn't touch, the coefficient goes to one.

Maya: Meaning it tells you nothing.

Sam: Nothing at all. If a reference system already distinguishes the two scenarios perfectly, no amount of noise on the other half helps. The community's patch was the "complete" contraction coefficient, which sidesteps triviality by demanding the two scenarios look identical on the untouched part. Clean, but artificial — real side information isn't identical across hypotheses.

Maya: So Hirche, George, Nuradha and Wilde propose a third option.

Sam: A conditional coefficient: allow arbitrary quantum side information, but subtract off whatever distinguishability the side information was already providing on its own. You only credit the channel for the correlations it destroys. And the main theorem is that for relative entropy this thing exactly equals the contraction coefficient of conditional mutual information — the natural quantity for "how much correlation survives, given a memory."

Maya: Let me push on this. Defining a quantity so it equals the quantity you wanted is not obviously an achievement. What does the identification actually buy?

Sam: Two things, and the second is the one I'd bet on. First, the optimization collapses: the worst case is achieved with a reference system that's a single classical bit. You go from a supremum over unbounded quantum references to something almost tractable. Second — tensorization. Ordinarily if you run many copies of a channel in parallel, relative-entropy contraction does not simply carry over; that failure is a known landmine. Here the conditional-mutual-information version tensorizes exactly.

Maya: So a bound proved for one use is automatically a bound for many uses in parallel.

Sam: Which is exactly what you need for networks. They also derive a quantum version of a classical relay-network bound from Polyanskiy and Wu — a broadcast step followed by a relay, where you interpolate between how much information survives with and without the extra leg. And there's a cute reframing of Lindblad dynamics they call replacing time: instead of asking when the state has mixed, you ask when the channel is honestly indistinguishable from one that just throws your state away and hands back a fixed one. They show it's asymptotically the same as mixing time.

Maya: Where's the soft ground?

Sam: One of the equivalences in their map is only proved one way — an inequality, not an identity. Computability is really only addressed through a Doeblin-style upper bound, which is an SDP, and it's the stronger version of a bound that was already known for the complete coefficient. And the mirror concept, expansion coefficients, stays trivially zero for essentially every divergence except trace distance. This is a toolmaker's paper. If you prove mixing times for Lindbladians or capacity bounds for relay networks, this is your new hammer.

[BREAK]

Maya: Paper three has a genuine puzzle at its center, and I want to state the puzzle before the solution. Lieb-Schultz-Mattis theorems say that if you have, say, a spin-one-half per site with translation and rotation symmetry, nature forbids a boring answer — you cannot have a unique gapped ground state. Fine for closed systems. Why has extending it to noisy, open systems been such a mess?

Sam: Because the obvious diagnostics fire for trivial reasons. In an open system your evolution is a channel applied over and over, and the object playing the role of the Hamiltonian has a spectrum. People said: look for degeneracy in the steady state, or look for gapless slow modes. But if your channel has a strong symmetry, trace preservation alone already forces steady-state degeneracy. And any conserved density already gives you slow hydrodynamic modes. Neither signals an anomaly.

Maya: So the alarm goes off whether or not there's a fire.

Sam: Exactly, and Gopalakrishnan, Liu, Lu, Pollmann and You fix it by changing which spectrum you look at — and how you look at the dynamics in the first place. The move is a spacetime duality: take repeated application of the channel and rotate the time direction into a spatial one. Now stacking many time steps builds a static two-dimensional state instead of a one-dimensional dynamics, and it's not just any state — it's a mixed-state symmetry-protected topological wavefunction, one dimension up.

Maya: And the input and output of the channel become...?

Sam: The physical edges of that higher-dimensional state. Which is lovely, because edges are where anomalies live, and we have decades of machinery for boundary modes of SPT phases. The bulk columns pair up into anomaly-free combinations and gap out; what's left dangling at the boundary carries the spin-one-half anomaly.

Maya: So what do you actually measure to see it?

Sam: Two things. One is a twisted correlator in the time direction, built from an operator charged under the strong symmetry — magnetization, say — and it maps onto what's called a strange correlator in the dual picture. Their claim is it can decay at most algebraically. It can't die exponentially. The second is sharper conceptually: they argue the anomaly shows up in the singular values of the transfer matrix, not its eigenvalues, and the constraint is that there can't be a single dominant singular vector.

Maya: That distinction sounds pedantic to a non-expert. Sell me on it.

Sam: The transfer matrix isn't Hermitian, so eigenvalues and singular values genuinely tell you different things — one governs long-time steady states, the other governs how much operator entanglement the dynamics carries. And in the dual language the singular spectrum is exactly the entanglement spectrum of that higher-dimensional SPT, which is the object that anomalies constrain. It's the right dictionary entry, which is why it's the faithful diagnostic.

Maya: What would make me doubt it?

Sam: A few things. That twisted correlator is quadratic in the state — you need two copies or postselection to measure it, so it's not walking onto a superconducting chip next month. Strange correlators are known to be imperfect witnesses; whether they show long-range order can depend on the trivial reference state you contract against, and that choice is a real input here. And the question of whether the strong symmetry survives the channel at all, versus degrading to a weak one, is pushed into an appendix — that could change the dual phase. But the reframing is clean, and tensor-network people can test it.

[BREAK]

Sam: Now, the history one. In two thousand and six, Kitaev conjectured that invertible phases of matter — the short-range-entangled ones, with nothing interesting inside but possibly something anomalous on the edge — should organize into a generalized cohomology theory. A tower where the phases in each dimension are the loops of the phases in the next.

Maya: That's a structural prediction, not a physical one.

Sam: It is, and separately there's a second classification problem that looks tantalizingly similar: quantum cellular automata, meaning unitary dynamics that strictly preserves locality — nothing propagates beyond a fixed distance in one step. And there's a third thread: the folklore that chiral phases, the ones with a net edge current, cannot be built from commuting local terms on a lattice.

Maya: Three separate stories that people keep noticing rhyme.

Sam: Jones, Sopenko and Thorngren argue the rhyming is because they're the same story, and the thing hiding it is an idealization we all make without thinking — finitely many degrees of freedom per lattice site. Allow each site to carry an infinite-dimensional operator algebra, a von Neumann algebra, and three groups become canonically isomorphic: cellular automata in one dimension higher, invertible phases in that same higher dimension, and a Brauer-style group of invertible algebras on the original space.

Maya: Okay, I have to push. Infinitely many degrees of freedom per site sounds like the mathematician's escape hatch. Why is that more physical than a qubit per site?

Sam: I had the same reaction, and the answer that moved me is that the infinite-dimensionality isn't optional. The bridge in their proof is a boundary algebra you attach to an invertible state, and that algebra comes out locally infinite-dimensional even when you started from an ordinary spin system. The finite-dimensional setting was never closed under the operations you want to perform. Stabilizing — allowing extra degrees of freedom — kills some cellular automata that looked exotic and creates others.

Maya: And they don't just prove an isomorphism, they populate it.

Sam: That's what makes it more than bookkeeping. They pull in rigorous algebraic quantum field theory — chiral conformal nets — and show that the discretized Majorana net and the E-eight net are invertible in their sense and nontrivial in the group. So there exist nontrivial invertible states and nontrivial cellular automata in two spatial dimensions, coming from honest field theories. And it follows that chiral phases do admit commuting local Hamiltonians, once you allow unbounded terms on infinite-dimensional sites.

Maya: Which contradicts the folklore.

Sam: It refines it — the obstruction was an artifact of the finite-dimensional setup. Taking K-theory of their category then produces the tower Kitaev conjectured, as an algebraic construction rather than an analytic one.

Maya: What's unproven?

Sam: The clean statement everyone wants — that the two-dimensional group is the integers, generated by the E-eight phase — is still a conjecture. Nontriviality is established through central charges that aren't divisible by three, so the fully general chiral case is deferred. The field theory inputs are all one-plus-one dimensional. And whether the strange three-dimensional automata people have found survive stabilization is open. This is a paper for mathematical physicists, but the punchline — a criterion for when a quantum field theory can live on a lattice at all — should interest everyone.

[BREAK]

Maya: Last one is the most immediately practical, and it starts with a device you can buy. Semi-device-independent random number generators: you prepare optical states, you don't trust the boxes, but you do monitor the mean photon number, and from the correlations you certify how many bits an eavesdropper can't predict.

Sam: They're attractive precisely because energy is easy to bound with off-the-shelf optics — no dimension assumptions, no detector characterization. The problem is that every security proof assumed the preparation box and the measurement box share only classical correlations. And recent work showed that's a real hole: an adversary who secretly entangles the two boxes before you switch them on does strictly better.

Maya: So all the certified rates evaporate.

Sam: All of them, and the open question was whether any randomness survives at all, or whether pre-shared entanglement kills the whole scheme. Ranu, Wooltorton, Abbott and Fawzi show randomness survives, and — this is the part that matters — they show it with a certified bound, not a heuristic search.

Maya: Explain the difference, because that word "certified" is doing a lot of work.

Sam: Seesaw optimization finds you an attack strategy, which gives an upper bound on the randomness: you know the adversary can do at least this well. That's not security. Security needs the other direction — a proof that no strategy in an infinite space does better. The standard tool for that is a semidefinite hierarchy in the style of Navascués, Pironio and Acín, which works when the parties are separated. Here they aren't: the receiver's measurement acts jointly on the transmitted message and on an ancilla the adversary supplied.

Maya: Which is exactly the structure the hierarchy was built to exclude.

Sam: So they decompose that joint measurement in the source basis, keep the low-dimensional block explicitly as a handful of operators, and lump absolutely everything else into a single remainder operator that obeys relations holding in any dimension. The projectivity condition gets imposed at the level of moment matrices rather than exactly — a deliberate, conservative weakening.

Maya: And the numbers?

Sam: With no dimension assumption at all, they certify strictly positive min-entropy for mean photon numbers up to about zero point one eight. Their own seesaw attacks stay positive out to roughly zero point three two, so there's a visible gap. They're honest that the gap is probably slack in the relaxation rather than clever high-dimensional attacks — the qubit and qutrit curves nearly coincide, which is a tell. There's also decent noise tolerance: in the low-energy regime the observed correlator can fall to a bit under sixty percent of ideal and you still get bits.

Maya: This sits interestingly next to the QKD key-rate paper we did on Tuesday, where the goal was escaping semidefinite programming to get key rates fast.

Sam: Opposite directions, same tension. That work was about speed for a well-understood scenario; this is about buying rigor in a scenario where nothing was rigorous, and paying for it with a level-two hierarchy on a solver. The honest caveats: this is single-round, independent-and-identically-distributed min-entropy, so no finite-size security statement yet. Everything is finite-dimensional while photonics really isn't. And they assume both preparations carry the same energy bound.

Maya: Still — the difference between "we think it's fine" and "we can prove something survives" is the whole ballgame for a deployed device.
