Welcome to The SciRate Daily Digest for Wednesday, August nineteenth, twenty twenty-six. I'm your host, and today we have a genuinely broad slate. We start deep in the algebra of quantum error correction, with a framework that finally lets non-CSS codes join the party. Then we swing through Hamiltonian simulation of slowly varying systems, sample-optimal entropy estimation, T-count records for arbitrary unitaries, classical simulation of bosonic circuits beyond Gaussian dynamics, neutral-atom compilation for bivariate bicycle codes, a fifty-year-old open problem in complex Hadamard matrices, Clifford deformation under biased noise, a foundational result about what superposition really means, and finally, a new proof of the PCP theorem that fits on a couple of pages. Let's get into it.

At number one today: "Non-CSS Quantum Code Embedding," by Andrew C. Yuan and Nouédyn Baspin.

Here's the situation this paper addresses. Almost all of the modern machinery for doing logical operations on quantum LDPC codes — surgery with ancilla systems, weight reduction, the three-dimensional embeddings that give you layer codes — is built on the language of chain complexes over the binary field. And chain complexes, fundamentally, describe CSS codes: codes where the X-type and Z-type checks live in separate worlds. If you have a non-CSS code, or even just a CSS code where you want to measure a Y-type logical operator, you're forced to conjugate by local Clifford gates to fake CSS structure, and that can wreck the LDPC property or the geometric locality you were relying on.

The move here is elegant. Instead of a chain complex, you use what the authors call a symplectic complex: a map sigma from a stabilizer space into the Pauli space, followed by sigma transpose composed with the symplectic form. The composition of those two maps being zero is exactly the statement that your stabilizers commute. That's the whole trick — the commutation condition becomes the boundary condition. Once you have that, cones, chain maps, and adjoints all carry over, and the CSS chain complex falls out as a special case.

The payoff is a single embedding theorem, about two pages, that transports cone-based constructions to arbitrary stabilizer codes. Direct Y-type logical measurement by cone construction. Non-CSS qLDPC surgery. Non-CSS weight reduction. Non-CSS layer codes. And they don't leave it abstract: there's an explicit circuit, one check qubit per stabilizer initialized in X, CNOTs implementing the X sector of the chain map and controlled-Z gates implementing the Z sector. The error propagation matrix comes out lower triangular, which means errors on check qubits become measurement errors, exactly as in ordinary CSS syndrome extraction. There's a genuinely non-CSS correction term in the logical readout coming from what they call the defect map, measuring the failure of the adjoint condition.

Fault tolerance follows from repeated rounds plus minimum-weight decoding on a spacetime fault complex. Now, the caveats: much of the concrete material lives in appendices, and the paper as visible doesn't nail down parameters for non-CSS layer codes or weight-reduction overheads. Thresholds are asserted via the standard Gottesman argument rather than simulated. And whether the resulting deformed codes stay LDPC with good distance in general is left open. Still, if you're designing qLDPC architectures or writing a fault-tolerant compiler, this is a framework you'll want on your shelf.

Moving to number two: "Quantum simulation of slow analytic time-dependent Hamiltonians," by Chenhao Zhao, Yinan Li, and Dong An.

The setting is Hamiltonians that vary slowly — think adiabatic schedules, annealing, quantum control — where the Hamiltonian at time t is really a fixed function of t divided by T, and T is large. The question is how the cost of simulation scales with the total time T and the target error epsilon. The prize here is additive rather than multiplicative scaling: they achieve query complexity of order alpha times T plus the logarithm of one over epsilon, up to polylogs, while keeping the extra gate overhead only polylogarithmic in one over epsilon.

The route is Floquet theory. If your Hamiltonian were periodic, you could Fourier expand, embed into a time-independent effective Hamiltonian on an enlarged index space, truncate the Fourier modes, block encode via linear combination of unitaries, and finish with quantum singular value transformation. The problem is that your Hamiltonian isn't periodic — it's defined on an interval. So the technical heart of the paper is constructing a periodic extension that matches all derivatives at the seam. You cannot do this analytically, by the identity theorem, so they work in Gevrey classes instead, building the extension from endpoint Taylor data damped by carefully scaled cutoff functions, and proving a quantitative version of the Borel lemma with explicit constants.

Because the extension is Gevrey but not analytic, the existing analysis for the analytic periodic case doesn't apply, so they prove fresh Fourier decay bounds and a Lieb-Robinson-type estimate to control the Floquet truncation order. The result also feeds into differential equation solving: combined with optimal-scaling linear combination of Hamiltonian simulation, you get slow semi-dissipative ODEs with a quadratic improvement in the logarithm of one over epsilon compared with prior time-dependent results.

The honest caveats: the input model is nonstandard, requiring block encodings of endpoint derivatives up to logarithmic order, so it's not an improvement under the plain HAM-T oracle. The gate cost is quadratic in alpha T, worse than truncated Dyson. And Gevrey classes of index two and above are excluded.

Number three: "Nearly Sample-Optimal Estimators for Quantum Rényi and Tsallis Entropies," by Kean Chen and Qisheng Wang.

This one closes gaps in a table that people have been filling in for years. How many copies of an unknown d-dimensional quantum state do you need to estimate its Rényi entropy of order alpha to within epsilon? For non-integer alpha above one, the previous best was order d squared over epsilon squared. This paper brings it to d squared over epsilon to the power one over alpha, plus a second term, matching recent lower bounds up to polylogarithmic factors. And for alpha between zero and one, the improvement is dramatic — from d to the two over alpha over epsilon to the two over alpha, down to d to the one plus one over alpha over epsilon to the one over alpha. That's a large polynomial speedup.

The key technical object is a new tangent-line inequality relating the alpha-Rényi functional to the chi-squared divergence. Roughly: if your estimate sigma is not too small, the error you make by linearizing the trace of rho to the alpha around sigma is bounded by the chi-squared divergence between rho and sigma, scaled by a dimensional factor. So accurate chi-squared tomography converts directly into a low-bias functional estimator. They then show that taking the output of known chi-squared tomography and regularizing it by adding a small multiple of the identity gives chi-squared divergence of order d squared over n.

For alpha below one, the algorithm is: spend part of your copies on tomography, compute one term classically, and estimate the remaining term by single-copy measurements in the eigenbasis of your estimate. For alpha above one, they take a different route — randomly purify the state, apply Hayashi's covariant pure-state POVM in batches, expand the bias in Beta-distribution moments, and use Richardson extrapolation across geometrically spaced batch sizes to cancel all the leading bias terms.

Methodologically the striking thing is that neither estimator uses weak Schur sampling, unlike every prior approach. Caveats: constants blow up as alpha approaches one from below, only constant success probability is proven, and collective measurements on many copies are still required.

Number four: "Quantum Circuit for General Unitary: Improved T-count via Block Flattening and Dilation," by Pei Yuan, Shengyu Zhang, and Wei Zi.

How many T gates does it take to implement an arbitrary n-qubit unitary? With d equal to two to the n, and L equal to n plus the logarithm of one over epsilon, the previous record from Tan was of order d to the four-thirds times L to the two-thirds. This paper gets d to the five-fourths times L to the five-eighths, times a log factor — an improvement whenever L is at most d. Against the known lower bound of order d, that narrows the exponent gap from one-third to one-quarter.

The conceptual shift is to stop decomposing the unitary into a product of simpler gates, and instead build one block encoding of the entire matrix. Three ingredients. First, block flattening: sandwich the unitary between two random sign diagonals and Hadamard transforms, and prove that every single b-by-b block of the result has spectral norm of order the square root of b over d, times a log. That's a uniform bound over all blocks, not just typical ones, and it's proved by Hoeffding plus a union bound to flatten entries, then a Tropp matrix Rademacher bound for the block norms.

Second, dilation: each flattened block is unitarily dilated using the Julia-Halmos construction, and all of them are compiled jointly into a single uniformly-controlled unitary — one SELECT operation. Third, amplification: a quantum singular value transformation polynomial that maps the single relevant singular value exactly to one, with an explicit perturbation analysis.

Balancing block size against SELECT cost gives the final exponent. The trade: ancilla count goes up substantially, to order d times the square root of L, versus Tan's much smaller footprint, and the space-time tradeoff isn't explored. The sign diagonals are found by a classical randomized search that's explicitly excluded from the resource count. And the authors note LLM assistance in preparing the manuscript, with independent verification claimed.

Number five: "Lie-Algebraic Classical Simulation of Bosonic Systems Beyond Gaussian Dynamics," by Adelina Bärligea, Timothy Heightman, Jakob Kottmann, and Antonio Acín.

Lie-algebraic simulation, or g-sim, says: if your circuit's generators span a polynomially-sized Lie algebra, you can propagate observables classically in that algebra and never touch the exponentially large Hilbert space. That works beautifully for spins and fermions. Bosons break it, because the natural algebras are infinite-dimensional and the operators unbounded.

The fix here is to stop asking about the dynamical Lie algebra and instead ask about what they call the observable-seeded reachable module: the smallest real operator space containing your observable that's invariant under commutation with each Hamiltonian term. If that space has polynomial dimension, and you can access input overlaps, then expectation values, multi-time correlators, out-of-time-order correlators, and even reverse-mode gradients all reduce to finite matrix algebra — no Fock cutoff, no moment closure.

Crucially, this is strictly more general. The standard condition is the special case where your observable sits inside the algebra, and they show it's sufficient but not necessary: linear observables under Gaussian dynamics need only two n plus one dimensions, versus order n squared for the full Gaussian algebra.

They identify three mechanisms that give finiteness. Degree preservation under quadratic Hamiltonians, which works even for non-Gaussian input states as long as you know the input moment tensor. Photon-number sector confinement, which makes self- and cross-Kerr interactions, number-diagonal terms, and pair hopping exactly simulable at arbitrary coupling and polynomial depth, provided the maximum photon number is constant. And a nilpotent cubic-and-higher phase family.

There's also a sharp negative result with no analogue in spin systems: a finite-dimensional generator algebra can still produce an infinite-dimensional observable orbit. Simulability is a property of the pair — dynamics and observable — not of the dynamics alone. Numerically they push operator spreading and OTOCs on chains up to four hundred modes, and study doublon bands with flux-reversed edge motion. And it's all consistent with boson sampling hardness, which lives in high-order coincidence observables, not mean values.

Number six: "Hardware-Aware Compilation and Execution of Bivariate Bicycle Codes on Neutral-Atom Systems," by Jason Ludmir and colleagues.

This is systems work, and it's addressing a real bottleneck. Bivariate bicycle codes are the leading candidate for near-term qLDPC memory, and there's now a reasonably well-defined logical instruction set for them: idles, shift automorphisms, in-module and inter-module logical measurements, T injection. Neutral atom arrays are the natural hardware because you can physically move qubits. But you can't move them arbitrarily — acousto-optic deflector transport imposes non-crossing constraints, you have blockade radii, and you have zoning.

Their system, Park-n-Ride, lays out compute zones as columns. Each column stacks code modules with idle, bridge-interaction, and shift-measurement subzones, plus a per-module logical processing unit holding bridge and ancilla atoms. The point is that almost all motion becomes column-local and therefore legal.

The main lever is placement. Which modules go in which column? They build the joint-measurement interaction graph and do spectral seriation — take the Fiedler vector, sort by it, and you get a one-dimensional ordering where frequently-interacting modules end up close together. Compared to arbitrary placement on schedules with up to one hundred thirteen gross-code modules, roughly thirty-two thousand physical qubits, that cuts estimated end-to-end runtime by about eighteen percent and expected transport-induced atom loss events by forty-one percent. Notably, spectral placement at column capacity six beats arbitrary placement at capacity ten — placement quality substituting for hardware.

They also have a shift-automorphism scheduler exploiting torus periodicity so all modules in a column roll in the same direction in parallel, and an interval-coloring scheduler for bridge qubits that guarantees non-crossing Bell pair formation via midpoint rendezvous.

Caveats are significant: no prior-art baseline, all runtimes come from an analytic estimator rather than circuit simulation, and logical error is a crude oracle-decoder binomial rather than decoded Monte Carlo.

Number seven, and a change of flavor: "A Complete Classification of Complex Hadamard Matrices of Order Six," by Mateo Cárdenes Wuttig and Joseph Tindall.

Complex Hadamard matrices — unimodular entries, orthogonal rows — are classified in every dimension up to five. Dimension six has been open for decades, and it matters, because it's the first dimension where continuous families and isolated points coexist, and because it blocks progress on the mutually unbiased bases problem in dimension six and on six-mode interferometers.

Szöllősi proposed a dilation method: take a three-by-three corner of the matrix, and if the set of matrices with that corner and the right Gram data is finite, you can reconstruct the whole thing by solving a finite algebraic system. What was missing was a global argument that such a good corner always exists. This paper supplies it.

The tool is a cubic Gram invariant: multiply together three off-diagonal entries of the Gram matrix in a cycle. They prove the row and column versions have equal real parts. Then comes the trichotomy: if a three-by-three unimodular matrix has an infinite fiber, then either its Gram is three times the identity, or the real part of its invariant is negative, or it contains a two-by-two Hadamard block. And then the routing argument: for complementary corners of a six-by-six Hadamard matrix, the off-diagonal Gram entries flip sign, so the two invariants are negatives of each other. One of them must be positive. Therefore one corner has finite fibers in both directions. That's the missing global step, and it proves Szöllősi's conjecture.

The remaining Fourier-block case — where some three-by-three block is itself Hadamard — resolves completely into Karlsson's three-parameter family plus Tao's isolated matrix. Parts are computer-certified: two hundred forty-five exact resultants and a tensor Bernstein positivity certificate for the Karlsson family. Fair warning on what "classification" means here: it's finite reconstructibility, not an explicit list of classes.

Number eight: "Computationally Efficient Optimization of Per-Qubit Clifford Deformation for Non-uniform Biased Noise," by Won Joon Yun, Andrew Nemec, and Jonathan Baker.

Real devices have biased noise, and the bias varies qubit to qubit. You can exploit that by choosing a single-qubit Clifford frame per qubit — essentially permuting which Pauli axis is which — leaving the code, the distance, and the syndrome schedule untouched. The problem is finding good frames, because evaluating a candidate normally means running Monte Carlo decoding.

Chameleon replaces that with an analytic surrogate. For a given logical Pauli operator that a decoder could confuse with the identity, the failure probability is upper bounded by a product over the qubits in its support of a Bhattacharyya-style factor, two times the square root of r times one minus r. That factorizes, so the cost per operator drops from exponential in the weight to linear. Sum over an enumerated set of low-weight logical representatives, and you have an objective you can evaluate in microseconds.

They then run a two-stage cross-entropy-method search: first over just identity versus Hadamard frames, then warm-started over all six frames, deployed only if it wins by a clear margin. On the seventy-two-qubit bivariate bicycle code, frame selection drops from about one point two days to three point one minutes, with worst-axis logical error rate reduced by twenty-one to thirty-two percent over the best prior baseline on Willow-derived noise maps.

The interpretation is nice: minus the logarithm of that Bhattacharyya factor is a per-qubit edge weight, so minimizing the objective is raising the effective weighted distance of the most fragile confusions. That's why the naive greedy rule — just suppress each qubit's dominant axis — actually underperforms plain CSS on twenty-seven percent of instances. Caveats: mostly phenomenological noise, circuit-level gains are only about five percent, and gains concentrate on strongly biased synthetic noise fields.

Number nine, and now for foundations: "No extension of the Quantum Tensor Product admits a Superposition principle," by Vincenzo Fiorentino and Kuntal Sengupta.

Superposition is invoked constantly — in proposals to test whether gravity is non-classical, in indefinite causal order experiments — but it's usually defined by writing a vector as a sum of basis vectors, which presupposes Hilbert space. This paper gives a Hilbert-space-free, prepare-and-measure definition inside generalized probabilistic theories. Take a maximal perfectly discriminable set of states and the extremal measurement that best distinguishes them. An extremal state is a superposition of a subset if it gives probabilistic outcomes on exactly those outcomes and nothing else. Maximality matters — they give a qutrit example where non-maximal discriminable sets produce spurious attributions.

From that they distil three principles: complete, uniform, and mutual superposition. And they show with two toy theories that these don't form a hierarchy.

The headline theorem: consider two quantum subsystems, and any non-signalling composition strictly between the quantum tensor product and the maximal tensor product. None of them admits mutual superposition. With the no-restriction hypothesis, no strict extension satisfies all three principles. So superposition, operationally defined, singles out the quantum tensor product — given quantum parts.

They also map the landscape. Classical theory and generalized local theory have no superposition. Boxworld has superposition — the PR box is a superposition of four separable states — but nothing stronger. Spekkens' toy theory satisfies all three principles and strong preparation uncertainty. So superposition is not the quantum-classical dividing line. Entanglement implies superposition; the converse fails.

Caveats: quantum subsystems are assumed, finite dimension, no-restriction hypothesis, and several results need multiplicativity of operational dimension, which fails in general.

And finally, number ten: "A Simple Algebraic Proof of the PCP Theorem," by Prashanth Amireddy, Amik Raj Behera, Srikanth Srinivasan, Madhu Sudan, and Sophus Valentin Willumsgaard.

The PCP theorem — that every NP statement has a proof checkable by reading a constant number of bits using logarithmically many random bits — has two standard proofs, and both are hard. The algebraic route needs proof composition, with all its robustification and parallelization and PCPs of proximity. This paper eliminates composition entirely, replacing it with plain code concatenation. Verifier and honest prover each fit in about a page of pseudocode.

Here's the obstruction they clear. In the algebraic construction, you can encode field-valued proof symbols using a Hadamard long code, so that constant-symbol queries become constant-bit queries. That works for everything except the lines table used in low-degree testing, whose entries are polylogarithmically many bits — far too big to long-code without blowing randomness up to quasipolynomial.

Their fix is a new encoding they call set-multilinear. Take a univariate polynomial of degree d, and map it to a set-multilinear polynomial in c times d-to-the-one-over-c variables, of total degree at most c, in such a way that evaluating along an explicit moment-curve-like map recovers the original. Now instead of storing each line's polynomial as coefficients, you store the evaluation table of its set-multilinear image, and you test it as a low-degree multivariate polynomial with constant queries via Rubinfeld-Sudan, self-correcting through an interpolation identity. Passing only guarantees closeness to some degree-c multivariate polynomial, which pulls back to a univariate of somewhat larger degree — weaker, but enough for soundness.

They also note no invocation of Cook-Levin inside the verifier construction. Caveats: the low-degree test still uses an auxiliary oracle, which the authors concede might count as, in Goldreich's phrase, half a composition; parameters aren't optimized; and simplicity remains an informal claim measured in pages.
