Welcome to the SciRate Daily Digest for Wednesday, August nineteenth, twenty twenty-six. I'm your host, and today we have a lineup that runs from photonics benches to operator inequalities. We'll look at a spatial light modulator for cold atoms that switches patterns in about twelve nanoseconds, a pair of nearly sample-optimal estimators that finally settle the cost of measuring quantum Rényi and Tsallis entropies, a rescue mission for curvature-based training of recurrent neural quantum states, a fermionic transport method that keeps only a handful of reservoir modes while bounding the error on the full state, and finally an analytic theory of how quasiparticle poisoning kills Majorana tetron qubits. Let's get into it.

At number one today, "Ultrafast and high resolution spatial light modulation for cold atoms," by Alexander Deters and colleagues, with Yanfei Li, Alexander Douglas, Markus Greiner, and Aaron Young.

Here's the problem this solves. If you want to paint arbitrary light patterns onto an array of trapped atoms, you currently have two bad options. Digital micromirror devices and liquid crystal displays give you lots of pixels, but they update on microsecond to millisecond timescales. Crossed acousto-optic deflectors are fast, but they're stuck producing outer products — the pattern you can make in two dimensions is essentially the product of a row pattern and a column pattern, not an arbitrary image.

The trick here is what the authors call a dispersive spatial light modulator. You cascade a virtually imaged phased array — a VIPA, which gives you fine angular resolution but a small free spectral range — with a regular diffraction grating, which gives you coarse resolution over a huge range. The combination maps optical frequency onto a two-dimensional position, like a raster scan written in color. So now, if you can modulate frequency and amplitude quickly, you can paint arbitrary patterns quickly. And modulating frequency and amplitude quickly is exactly what the telecom industry has spent thirty years perfecting.

The numbers are striking. Rise times of about twelve nanoseconds, which is over eighty-four million frames per second. Eighty-three by fifty-two resolvable beam waists across the field of view, with eleven by fifty-two accessible through a single forty gigahertz Mach-Zehnder modulator. Intensity homogeneity at the level of one part in a thousand. And because the more than ten terahertz of available telecom bandwidth is being converted into spatial resolution, pattern complexity is decoupled from any pixel count.

There's a lovely bit of engineering hygiene here too. The VIPA's polishing imperfections introduce aberrations, but those aberrations turn out to be nearly frequency-independent, so a single static liquid crystal correction recovers diffraction-limited spots across the whole band. Adjacent spots are separated by at least fifty megahertz in one axis or a full free spectral range in the other, so interference between them time-averages away on atomic timescales — that's what buys the one-part-in-a-thousand intensity control. And there's a cute trick: a pi phase jump extinguishes a spot faster than light can even transit the VIPA.

What can you do with it? They demonstrate continuous two-dimensional motion, chirping within a free spectral range to move vertically and handing amplitude between tones to move horizontally, at up to about a hundred waists per microsecond with resolution essentially preserved. They braid and permute five spots arbitrarily in about one point two microseconds, and argue for sublinear-time arbitrary two-dimensional permutations — which matters because that would enable quantum LDPC codes whose connectivity is incompatible with acousto-optic routing. They also lay out a numerically validated scheme for fully programmable square-lattice Hubbard models: base tweezers set the chemical potentials, interlaced barrier tweezers set the hoppings, curvature tweezers set the on-site interactions, all optimized jointly, with per-link Floquet drive phases generating arbitrary plaquette flux patterns.

The honest caveats: there are no atoms yet. Everything is optical bench characterization, and the figures rely on post-processing that stands in for optics they haven't built — stitching together free spectral ranges, cropping the carrier and negative sidebands because single-sideband modulation wasn't available, stretching one axis for ellipticity. Throughput is currently five point eight percent, though they claim above fifty percent with custom optics. And scaling that one-part-in-a-thousand homogeneity beyond three tones will demand very linear, high dynamic range wideband RF. Still, a scaling study with a sixty centimeter VIPA suggests over a thousand by a thousand waists is on the table. Speed has been the binding constraint for Floquet engineering and fast quench protocols; this is a serious attempt to remove it.

From photons to statistics. At number two, "Nearly Sample-Optimal Estimators for Quantum Rényi and Tsallis Entropies," by Kean Chen and Qisheng Wang.

The question is simple to state: given copies of an unknown d-dimensional quantum state, how many do you need to estimate its Rényi entropy of order alpha, or its Tsallis entropy, to within epsilon? Recent work by Wang established lower bounds. This paper supplies matching upper bounds, up to polylogarithmic factors, for all non-integer orders. The improvements are substantial. For alpha between zero and one, the cost drops from d to the two-over-alpha over epsilon to the two-over-alpha, down to roughly d to the one-plus-one-over-alpha over epsilon to the one-over-alpha. For non-integer alpha greater than one, Rényi estimation drops from d squared over epsilon squared to d squared over epsilon to the one-over-alpha, plus a lower-order term.

What's methodologically interesting is that both estimators abandon weak Schur sampling, the workhorse of essentially all prior work. For alpha below one, they run Bures chi-squared tomography, regularize the estimate by adding two d over n times the identity, and then compute a linearized surrogate for the trace of rho to the alpha. The bias of that surrogate is controlled by a new operator inequality the authors prove: the concavity gap is sandwiched between zero and a factor times the Bures chi-squared divergence between the true state and the regularized estimate. And they show that regularized Bures chi-squared tomography gives a chi-squared divergence of order d squared over n. That inequality feels like a reusable tool.

For alpha above one, the construction is even prettier. Random purification turns copies of rho into copies of a Haar-random purification, Hayashi's covariant pure-state POVM is applied, and the resulting estimator's bias has a clean expansion in moments of a Beta distribution. So they run several geometrically spaced batch sizes and solve a small linear system to cancel all the polynomial bias terms — Richardson extrapolation, borrowed from numerical analysis, deployed against quantum estimator bias.

Caveats worth flagging: constants blow up as alpha approaches one; the estimators require collective measurements on all n copies; computational cost isn't analyzed; and success probability is only constant, so you'd want median-of-means for confidence. But the landscape for entropies built on the trace of rho to the alpha is now essentially settled.

Next, from theory to practice in variational quantum states. Number three: "Quantum Geometric Tensor Preconditioning for Stable Training of Recurrent Neural Quantum States," by Adil Attar, Amine Aboussalah, and Mohamed Hibat-Allah.

The received wisdom has been that recurrent neural network wave functions just don't get along with curvature-based optimizers like stochastic reconfiguration — the quantum geometric tensor is too ill-conditioned, and people fall back to Adam. This paper says: not so fast. Using minimum-step stochastic reconfiguration, which moves the matrix inversion into the sample space rather than parameter space, plus nothing more exotic than a constant Tikhonov shift and momentum, they train gated recurrent unit wave functions stably. On the one-dimensional transverse field Ising model at two hundred sites, and the one-dimensional cluster state at one hundred sites, they beat Adam by orders of magnitude in relative energy error — hitting minus ninety-nine point nine nine against an exact minus one hundred, with only one to two hundred samples on a single GPU.

But they're refreshingly honest about where it stops working. In two dimensions, on the square-lattice Heisenberg model and the frustrated J-one J-two model at the maximally frustrated point, minimum-step stochastic reconfiguration is merely comparable to Adam. They trace this to a genuinely more ill-conditioned landscape, and they visualize it: the convex basin along the extremal eigenvectors of the geometric tensor is about ten times narrower in the two-dimensional case.

There's real structure here too. They prove two propositions for a linear recurrent network with a softmax head. First, there's an exact general linear group gauge freedom in the hidden basis, and its orbit velocities lie in the kernel of the geometric tensor for any sample set — so the matrix is intrinsically singular, not accidentally so. Second, recurrent amplification makes the largest eigenvalue grow like N cubed for a marginal mode, and exponentially in the sequence length for expanding modes. That's a clean structural explanation of the ill-conditioning, likely relevant to gated architectures generally, even though the authors are careful to note that a GRU need not carry that full symmetry exactly.

They also give a Markov-inequality bound showing that subsampling the geometric tensor still yields a useful preconditioner even when the sample count is far below the parameter count, and they report negative results on Levenberg-Marquardt damping, diagonal preconditioning, and structural damping. The practical takeaway they suggest: use curvature methods at small sizes, then transfer to large sizes and switch to Adam.

Turning now to open quantum system dynamics. At number four: "Long-time fermionic quantum transport with controlled full-state error using an adaptive reservoir-mode window," by Mikhail Umanskii and colleagues.

The scenario is a small device coupled to leads, and you want to simulate the transport for a long time. The reservoir is huge, so you need to throw modes away — but which ones, and what does that cost you? This paper extends what the authors call tape-recorder coarse graining from bosons to fermions. The picture is a moving window of reservoir modes: modes that haven't started coupling yet are queued up ahead, modes that have finished coupling are ejected behind, and you only carry the active ones.

The key technical move for fermions is a particle-hole mapping of the filled left lead into a quasiparticle vacuum, so both leads start from vacuum and the light cone in mode space is preserved. Anticommutation is handled by an identity that lets the error bound avoid any assumption about the occupation of the outgoing mode being discarded. The bound itself comes from an exact Duhamel identity, not perturbation theory, and — this is the selling point — it bounds the infidelity of the full device-plus-reservoir state, not just the device's reduced density matrix.

Numerically, the payoff is dramatic. The active mode count per lead saturates in time: five to six modes at a cutoff of ten to the minus four, all the way out to time three thousand. And the window width grows only logarithmically as you tighten the threshold — about zero point seven times the log of one over the cutoff. They benchmark an interacting, driven two-site quantum point contact against hierarchical equations of motion, against Landauer-Büttiker, and against exact Floquet Green functions. The showcase case is a flat band with algebraically decaying correlations, where HEOM's exponential bath decomposition struggles to converge but this method reproduces Landauer-Büttiker cleanly.

Limitations: the bound is per discard event and grows with elapsed time, and accumulation over many discards isn't bounded. Production runs used a fixed rank cap rather than the threshold criterion, so convergence is ultimately empirical. And the setting is restricted — noninteracting leads, spinless fermions, zero temperature, a two-site device.

And finally, at number five: "Dynamics of Majorana tetron qubits under quasiparticle poisoning," by Sauri Bhattacharyya and Bernard van Heck.

A tetron is four Majorana zero modes on a charged island — the leading candidate architecture for a topological qubit. Its protection against quasiparticle poisoning is supposed to come from charging energy: adding an electron costs U, so poisoning rates carry a Boltzmann factor of e to the minus U over temperature. This paper works out, analytically, exactly how that protection degrades.

They derive a Bloch-Redfield master equation for a tetron tunnel-coupled to four normal leads, and get closed-form steady states, parity leakage rates, and decoherence rates at arbitrary ratios of charging energy to temperature and arbitrary Majorana splitting epsilon. The headline formula: the even-sector rate is approximately Gamma-zero times e to the minus beta U times the hyperbolic cosine of beta epsilon. So the exponential charging protection is progressively eaten away by the qubit energy splitting — once epsilon becomes comparable to U, or to temperature, you lose it. There's also a subtlety worth appreciating: the leakage amplitude is suppressed by e to the minus beta U, but it occurs at the unsuppressed rate Gamma-zero.

A nice structural result: for the symmetric model with identical uncorrelated leads, they prove the secular dissipator is exactly identical to the universal Lindblad equation dissipator. All the interference terms vanish, and the cross-Bohr-frequency recycling terms cancel when you sum over Majoranas in a canonical pair. That extends the validity of the simple formulas down to vanishing splitting.

And there's a potential experimental discriminant. If instead of genuine localized Majoranas you have an extended zero-energy Andreev state, the lead couplings become cross-correlated, even and odd coherences mix, and exponential decay becomes bi-exponential. Strikingly, in the fully delocalized limit, one branch actually slows down relative to the naive rate — delocalization makes poisoning-induced decoherence slower, which is the opposite of the intuition and could be a signature to look for.

Caveats: weak-coupling Born-Markov only, Lamb shifts discarded, leads assumed gapless and wide-band with equal tunnel rates, and above-gap intrinsic poisoning and one-over-f charge noise are excluded. But these are ready-to-fit time-domain formulas for parity lifetime and coherence experiments in indium arsenide-aluminum tetrons and quantum-dot Kitaev chain analogues.
