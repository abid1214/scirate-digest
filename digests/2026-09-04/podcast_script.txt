Sam: Not gone. Spread out so thin that no local observer could get at it. Which — and I promise this is not a stretch — is essentially the argument of our top paper today.

Sam: And I'm Sam. Today: chaos as an error-correcting code, the actual geometric shape of the set of states with no magic, lattice codes built backwards from the decoder, what pseudorandomness really requires, and a homology dictionary for logical gates.

Maya: No mailbag today, so we go straight in. Sam, take us to the thermalizing Hamiltonian.

[BREAK]

Sam: Right. So there's a tension people have gestured at for years and never really cashed out. Quantum chaos is described as scrambling — the system forgets its initial conditions. But it doesn't forget. Unitary evolution is reversible. The information is still there, it's just been smeared over the whole system so that no small piece of it tells you anything.

Maya: And that's the definition of an error-correcting code. If no small region has information about the logical state, you can lose that region without losing the qubit.

Sam: Exactly the move this paper makes. Venkatesh, Allen, Pilatowsky-Cameo, Ye and Choi take a generic chaotic Hamiltonian, let a bunch of orthogonal starting states evolve for a while, and ask: as a code, how good is the resulting subspace? And the answer isn't "decent". It's optimal.

Maya: Optimal against what standard? Because "optimal" in coding theory usually means you're bumping into a bound.

Sam: The bound here is the entropic version of the quantum Singleton bound — the fundamental ceiling on how many qubits you can protect against how much erasure, given how much entropy the system has to work with at that temperature. They extract thresholds numerically from several different chaotic models and they all collapse onto one curve, and that curve is the bound. So the tradeoff is clean: if you only spend half the system's entropy budget on encoded qubits, you can afford to lose a quarter of the system.

Maya: Hold on. How big are these simulations? Chaotic dynamics means exact diagonalization, which means small.

Sam: Twenty spins at the top end. That's the honest weakness — it's a finite-size scaling collapse over a modest range, and the universal curve is an extrapolation from it. What backs it up is the analytic side. They define what they call Scrooge codes, which are the thermal-state analogue of picking codewords at random, and they prove those hit the entropic bound exactly, under an assumption about how the energy spectrum concentrates.

Maya: So the numerics say ordinary chaotic dynamics behaves like the idealized random construction.

Sam: And that's the payoff, because the idealized random construction would take an exponentially deep circuit to build. Here your encoder is just... waiting. Let a many-body system evolve.

Maya: My objection is the other end of the pipeline. A code being information-theoretically optimal tells me the information is recoverable in principle. It tells me nothing about whether I can recover it before the heat death of the universe.

Sam: Completely fair, and they don't provide a decoder. Which is a nice hook back to Monday's episode — we covered two papers on the theory of approximate error correction, including universal recovery. That line of work says a single decoder can cover a whole noise family. This paper hands that machinery a new, physically preparable family of codes to chew on.

Maya: What about conservation laws? Real Hamiltonians conserve energy, and conserved quantities are exactly the thing a local observer can measure.

Sam: They handle it, and I liked the answer. Energy leakage is purely classical — an eavesdropper on a small region learns which energy window you're in and nothing more. And correctability survives as long as your codewords' energies differ by less than typical thermal fluctuations, which grow like the square root of system size.

Maya: Verdict?

Sam: Quantum chaos people should read it as a sharp operational diagnostic of ergodicity. Coding theorists should read it for the Scrooge construction. What I'd watch for is timescales — they're explicit that they don't pin down when correctability switches on, whether it tracks scrambling or full thermalization. And it's erasure noise only.

[BREAK]

Maya: Next one is about magic — the resource beyond stabilizer circuits, the thing magic state distillation exists to manufacture. And it's a geometry paper.

Sam: It is, and the framing I like is historical. We've known for twenty-plus years that the states with no magic — mixtures of stabilizer states — form a convex polytope. Everyone draws that polytope on a whiteboard. Almost nobody could tell you its actual shape.

Maya: Shape meaning what, concretely?

Sam: Three numbers. How big is the ball of nearly-maximally-mixed states guaranteed to sit inside it. How much total volume does it occupy. And how many flat faces does it have — because each face is a linear inequality, and linear inequalities are witnesses you could test in a lab.

Maya: And Liu and Liu answer all three.

Sam: The centerpiece is the ball. They prove a purity threshold that doesn't depend on dimension at all: if a state is mixed enough, by a very simple criterion, it is provably a mixture of stabilizer states. The constant they get is about point four six, where the conjectured optimum is a half. So the gap is now a constant, not a growing function.

Maya: That's the kind of criterion that's actually usable — I have a noisy state, I measure its purity, I know whether there's any magic to distill.

Sam: Right, and it feeds directly into the other two answers. Plug the ball into asymptotic convex geometry and you learn the polytope's volume is tiny — essentially as small as the ball's. And the face count is doubly exponential in the number of qubits.

Maya: Doubly exponential. So the dream of a complete set of magic witnesses is dead.

Sam: Dead in the strongest sense. You cannot write down the exact facet description of that polytope, ever, and this is a theorem, not a difficulty. Anyone hunting for exhaustive magic witnesses should redirect.

Maya: There's a result in here comparing magic and entanglement that I want you to unpack, because it surprised me.

Sam: Take a random state on your qubits plus some environment, and trace the environment out. As you make the environment bigger, your state gets more mixed and eventually loses its resources. Entanglement dies once the environment is about one and a half times the size of the system. Magic hangs on until the environment is about twice the size. So there's a whole regime of typical states that are separable — no entanglement at all — and still magical.

Maya: Which cuts against the folk intuition that magic is the fragile resource and entanglement is the robust one.

Sam: It does. Though the pushback writes itself: that's the Haar-random ensemble. Nobody's device produces Haar-random states with an environment attached. Gibbs states, shallow circuits — those are the physically motivated ensembles, and the authors flag them as open.

Maya: One more caveat I noticed: the inradius proof leans on a machine-checked exact rational computation out to very large dimension, plus a tail bound.

Sam: It does, and I don't think that's a flaw — it's rigorous arithmetic, not a simulation. But it means the remaining gap to the conjectured constant probably won't close by pushing the computation further. Someone needs a new idea. This one's for resource-theory people and anyone designing benchmarking protocols.

[BREAK]

Sam: Now, a paper with a very concrete stake. If you're building a bosonic quantum computer, every optical or microwave mode is expensive real estate. So: how many modes to protect one qubit?

Maya: And the standard recipe is grid states — GKP — concatenated with a surface code.

Sam: That's the recipe, and it works, but it throws something away. Encoding a qubit into oscillators is really just choosing a lattice in phase space, and correcting an error is finding the nearest lattice point. When you concatenate, you're forcing that lattice to factor into single-mode pieces glued by a qubit code, and you discard some of the continuous analog information the hardware handed you.

Maya: So why does everyone do it? Because nearest-lattice-point is hard?

Sam: It's the closest vector problem — the same hardness that post-quantum cryptography is built on. Native multimode grid codes have been almost unexplored purely because you can't decode them. So Hillmann, Eisert and Arzani invert the design order. They start from classical low-density lattice codes — the lattice cousin of LDPC codes, with a known fast approximate decoder — force them to satisfy the symplectic condition that makes them valid quantum codes, and then ask if the result is any good.

Maya: Decodability as a design constraint rather than an afterthought. What comes out?

Sam: The raw construction encodes an absurdly large logical dimension, so they run a dimension-reduction procedure — exact rational arithmetic, splitting one invariant factor across two extra rows — to collapse it down to a single qubit while raising the distance. And the reduced codes at fifteen to seventeen modes match or beat a twenty-five mode surface code baseline, with the gap widening as the noise gets weaker.

Maya: Fifteen against twenty-five is a real saving. But you just told me the whole point was designing for the decoder. Do the reduced codes still decode?

Sam: They don't, and this is the paper's own central irony. The two extra dense rows create short cycles in the graph, and message passing falls over. So the error rates they quote come from brute-force closest-vector search, which is only feasible out to about seventeen modes. The comparison is about intrinsic code quality, not about an operational decoder.

Maya: That's a big asterisk. And the noise model?

Sam: Idealized — independent Gaussian displacements, single shot, no finite squeezing, no measurement error. So this is a "there is something here worth chasing" paper rather than an architecture proposal.

Maya: What would make it real?

Sam: Somebody finding a message-passing schedule that survives those dense checks, or a reduction that doesn't need them. They also ship open source lattice decoder packages, and there's a nice side result: a purely analog message-passing decoder does give thresholds for simpler grid-repetition codes, though with error floors they trace to how they represent the messages, and they say plainly they don't yet know how to fix that in general.

Maya: Honest paper. Bosonic code designers should read it; hardware people can wait a version.

[BREAK]

Maya: Wednesday we covered depth-one expanders on the unitary group — scrambling as fast as random dynamics, in one layer. Today there's a paper that rhymes with that, on the computational side.

Sam: It really does. So there are two flavors of quantum randomness. Statistical: your ensemble mimics truly random dynamics for a fixed number of copies — that's a design. Computational: your ensemble only has to fool observers who run in reasonable time — that's a pseudorandom unitary. And every known construction of the computational object bolts cryptography on top of a statistical object.

Maya: Which begs the question of whether the statistical layer is load-bearing or just convenient.

Sam: That's the question Raza, Eisert and Fefferman answer. They isolate a much weaker property they call distinctness. Roughly: if you query the unitary several times and measure, you almost never get colliding outcomes — and crucially, that has to hold even when you feed in entangled inputs across the queries.

Maya: Why is it necessary? Give me the one-line argument.

Sam: It's a contrapositive, and it's almost too simple. Truly random dynamics essentially never produces collisions. So if your ensemble does produce them with non-negligible probability, looking for a collision is itself an efficient test that distinguishes you from random. You're not pseudorandom. Done.

Maya: Fine, necessary. Sufficient is the harder direction.

Sam: Within the standard template, they show it suffices — you can rip out the Clifford layer and drop in anything merely distinct. And they exhibit an ensemble that is distinct but so far from random that it maps the all-zeros state to one fixed state, deterministically, every single time. It isn't even a design at the very first level. It still drives the construction.

Maya: That's the result I'd put on the poster. It means anticoncentration and being a design aren't the same requirement — they just coincide in the architectures people happened to use.

Sam: And the practical dividend is the depth. The global Clifford layer costs you a logarithmic depth factor. A single layer of independent single-qubit Cliffords — depth one — is distinct enough. That's the echo of Wednesday's paper, from a completely different direction.

Maya: What's the test it gives you for free?

Sam: A cheap no-go. If a candidate construction fails distinctness, you get an explicit distinguisher out of Bell states. They use it to kill a standing conjecture about alternating phase-and-Hadamard constructions whenever the phase function's range is too small.

Maya: Where does this stop being true? Because "necessary and sufficient" makes me suspicious.

Sam: Two places, and they're upfront. Everything is non-adaptive, forward-query security — no adaptive adversaries, no access to the inverse. Nobody knows the right notion of distinctness in those settings. And the analysis breaks down once your query count approaches the square root of the Hilbert space dimension, where a known collision attack takes over anyway.

Maya: For cryptographers, then, this is a modularity result rather than a security upgrade.

Sam: That's the right framing. Pseudorandomness equals cheap collision suppression plus classical cryptography, instead of design plus cryptography. It also lets them build real-valued pseudorandom unitaries, secure on any input without too much Bell overlap — which covers every separable state.

[BREAK]

Sam: Last one, and I want to lead with the punchline because it's striking. Constant-depth Hadamard on a single logical qubit of a two-dimensional toric code. Addressable.

Maya: That's a gate people have historically needed elaborate scaffolding for — folding the whole patch, ancilla systems, code deformation.

Sam: Right. Sahay, Long and Khemani get it, plus the entire logical Clifford group across and within toric code blocks, plus an addressable controlled-controlled-Z on any triple of logical qubits across three-dimensional blocks. But the gates aren't the contribution. The framework is.

Maya: Which is?

Sam: For memory, we've had a beautiful dictionary for years: logical operators are homology classes, two circuits implementing the same logical operator differ by a boundary. Gates never had that. Gate constructions were a grab bag — transversal tricks, folding, cup products, surgery gadgets. What this paper shows is that the space of all physical circuits implementing a logical gate between two codes is itself the chain space of an auxiliary complex, and that complex is just the tensor product of the two codes.

Maya: So the gate problem becomes the memory problem, one level up.

Sam: Precisely. Circuits that preserve the code space are cycles. Circuits that act trivially on the logical information are boundaries. Which gate you get is a homology class. And then deforming a logical operator — the most familiar move in the business — deforms your circuit.

Maya: Make that concrete for me. What does a logical controlled-Z between two toric codes look like in this picture?

Sam: It's a sheet-like logical operator of the four-dimensional toric code. And you read the circuit straight off the sheet: every supported plaquette says "put a physical controlled-Z between this qubit of block one and that qubit of block two." Then they do something they call tenting — you pitch the sheet up like a tent, which is just an ordinary stabilizer deformation — and the gate that used to have depth growing with the code distance collapses to constant depth, and becomes addressable to a single logical qubit.

Maya: I have a serious worry. Constant depth is not the same as fault tolerant. Those controlled-Z gates are nonlocal, and a single fault in a constant-depth circuit can still spread to a lot of qubits.

Sam: That worry is correct and the paper doesn't resolve it. They analyze the algebra, not the error propagation, and the threshold behavior of these new toric code Cliffords is simply not addressed. There's also an extension of the standard no-go theorems to nonlocal constant-depth circuits, which they explicitly label informal.

Maya: And homology tells you a gate exists in some class. It doesn't hand you a sparse circuit.

Sam: That's the other limitation. Constant depth needs a sparse representative cycle, and finding one is still search and cleverness. Homology tells you where to look, not what you'll find. Also the framework covers controlled-Z and controlled-X style gates and a few intra-block ones — not arbitrary logical unitaries.

Maya: Still, for anyone designing architectures around high-rate codes, this changes the question you ask.

Sam: It turns "does a transversal gate exist here?" into a homology computation. That's a genuinely different working life. I'd read this alongside the qLDPC code-design work we've seen all week — those papers were about finding good blocks. This one is about making blocks talk.
